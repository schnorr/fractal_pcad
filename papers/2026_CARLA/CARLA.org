*** Context                                                        :ignore:
Modern computing is inherently parallel. From multi-core processors in
everyday devices to the hundreds of thousands of cores in
leadership-class supercomputers, parallelism is no longer an advanced
specialization: it is the default execution model. Nevertheless,
despite this reality, parallel computational thinking remains a
late-stage topic in most Computer Science curricula. Concepts such as
domain partitioning, task granularity, load balancing, speedup, and
scalability are routinely deferred to upper-division or graduate
electives, leaving students ill-equipped to reason about the hardware
they will inevitably program. This curricular gap begins even earlier:
prospective students arriving from high school have virtually no
exposure to parallel computational thinking, entering undergraduate
programs without even an informal intuition for the concepts that
professors will address years into the students' studies. This
situation is particularly consequential in the context of
High-Performance Computing (HPC), where understanding the interplay
between problem decomposition and machine architecture is a
prerequisite for productive scientific work.

A well-established strategy for bridging this gap is the use of
interactive, visually rich tools that make parallel phenomena
tangible. When a student can directly observe how domain partitioning
affects load balance, or watch speedup curves shift in response to
granularity choices, abstract concepts acquire intuitive
grounding. However, tools designed with this pedagogical goal in mind
are scarce. Existing HPC education frameworks assume prior programming
experience, offer limited real-time visual feedback, or present
architectures too complex to be useful in introductory settings.

*** Bridge to our strategy                                         :ignore:

This context motivated the development of two open-source applications
at the Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS). The
immediate driver was a concrete pedagogical event: the annual Open Day
at UFRGS, which welcomes prospective students, mostly high schoolers
with no formal computing background, for a first hands-on encounter
with Computer Science. This setting imposed strict design
constraints. The tools had to be immediately accessible, visually
compelling, and capable of conveying non-trivial parallel-computing
concepts during a brief, informal interaction. At the same time, the
same tools should scale in depth, remaining useful in formal
undergraduate instruction.

*** The two applications overview                                  :ignore:

The two selected problems are visually compelling, computationally
intensive, and pedagogically complementary. The first is the
Mandelbrot set, a canonical embarrassingly parallel problem. Each
pixel's computation is independent, making it an ideal entry point for
introducing domain decomposition and granularity without the cognitive
overhead of inter-process communication. Its visual richness is a key
asset: zooming in increases the resolution demands and redistributes
computational work across regions, allowing users to observe and
address load imbalance directly. The second application is an N-Body
gravitational simulation (galaxy simulation), which presents a
fundamentally different parallel structure. With O(N²) complexity and
mandatory communication phases between computation steps, it exposes
students to the challenges of tightly coupled parallelism:
synchronization, communication overhead, and the tension between
scalability and correctness. Together, the two problems span a
representative spectrum of parallel computing, from trivially
decomposable to communication-bound, with a visual interactive
framework.

The rendering and simulation processes thus become live experiments in
parallel computational thinking. Rather than reasoning about
parallelism through code inspection alone, users observe its effects
directly: partition granularity across the Mandelbrot canvas, particle
mappings for workers evolving under gravitational interaction, and
simulation speed responding to changes in process count and
granularity. Core concepts, such as domain decomposition, granularity,
load balance, speedup, efficiency, and scalability, emerge naturally
from interaction, grounded in immediate visual feedback.

*** Contribution Wrap-up                                           :ignore:

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
- Two open-source interactive tools for parallel computing education,
  built on an MPI-based architecture with real-time visualization;
- An explicit conceptual mapping between the interactive and visual
  elements of both tools and the core concepts of parallel
  computational thinking, namely domain partitioning, granularity,
  load balance, speedup, efficiency, and scalability;
- A pedagogical experience report covering two distinct audiences —
  Open Day visitors and undergraduate students — with qualitative
  assessment of engagement and conceptual uptake;
- Full public availability of source code, documentation, and
  supporting materials at \COMPANION.
